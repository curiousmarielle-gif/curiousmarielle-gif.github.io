•Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS)

Definition

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated allergy to galactose-α-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal), a carbohydrate epitope expressed in non-primate mammalian tissues.9

Diagnosis

A positive alpha-gal-specific IgE result indicates sensitization; diagnosis of AGS requires a compatible clinical history.3,9,10

Route

After ingestion of alpha-gal-containing mammalian products, reactions are typically delayed by 2 to 6 hours and may occur later. Parenteral reactions may be immediate.5,9

Implants can prolong exposure.5,9 A first mammalian product in the suite can be the index reaction.10,3

Food-challenge thresholds do not establish cosmetic-product safety.11

Epidemiology

  • 90,018 suspected U.S. cases, 2017-2022.12
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated 96,000-450,000 people may have been affected since 2010.12
  • Blood-donor seroprevalence, 300 samples per state: Arkansas 31.2% (95% confidence interval [CI], 25.7-36.6); Missouri 26.0% (95% CI, 22.0-30.0). Seropositivity is not clinical AGS.10

Cosmetic and aesthetic exposure

Hemostats, animal matrices, grafts, sutures, collagen carriers, heparin, hyaluronidase, implants, and some excipients can be mammalian-derived. Check source and the finished formulation.1,3,21,22

Do symptoms and clinical history suggest alpha-gal syndrome?

Tick exposure and endemic residence provide context but do not alone establish an indication for routine testing.

Tier 1 · Low clinical suspicion

No symptoms or clinical history suggestive of alpha-gal syndrome (AGS). Tick exposure or endemic residence alone remains in this category.

Action

  • Alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E screening is not indicated.10,12,30
  • No AGS-based material restriction.
  • Usual care; remain alert to reactions.

Why testing is not indicated

Sensitization is common. Testing without a compatible history increases false-positive labeling.10,12,30

Tier 2 · Suspected

Symptoms and clinical history suggest alpha-gal syndrome (AGS); diagnosis is not yet established.

Action

  • Order alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) testing and interpret the result with the clinical history. A positive alpha-gal-specific IgE result indicates sensitization; diagnosis of AGS requires a compatible clinical history.9,11,30
  • Involve allergy/immunology after a systemic or anaphylactic event.30,20
  • Prefer non-mammalian materials until results return.

Interpreting test results

≥0.1 kU/L is detectable and suggestive; <0.1 kU/L is negative; conventional positivity is commonly ≥0.35 kU/L. Read the titer with the history. Titer correlates imperfectly with reactivity and is not a cosmetic-product cutoff.9,11,30

Tier 3 · Established

Compatible history with positive alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE), commonly ≥0.35 kU/L.9,11,30

Action

  • Preferred material: source-verified non-mammalian substitutes when feasible.
  • Specialist involvement: allergy/immunology.
  • Emergency preparedness: epinephrine immediately available.37
  • When a non-mammalian option is unavailable: shared decision-making.

Material verification and clinical considerations
  • Check species, source, excipients, and lot against the current instructions for use before use.
  • Porcine unfractionated heparin: substitute if feasible; use caution with high-dose intravenous exposure. The cardiac-procedure evidence does not establish reaction rates for cosmetic procedures; see Materials, row MAT-HEP.4
  • If a mammalian-derived agent cannot be avoided, premedication (corticosteroid + antihistamine) is expert opinion only and does not replace epinephrine readiness.30,37

Intraprocedural verification

  1. IDENTIFY every mammalian-derived item on the field - hemostats, matrices, sutures, injectables, hyaluronidase, excipients.
  2. VERIFY species, source, excipients, and lot against the current instructions for use - not the brand name alone.
  3. SUBSTITUTE with a source-verified non-mammalian option where one exists.
  4. IF NO SUBSTITUTE - flag for allergy/immunology input and anaphylaxis precautions.

    Hyaluronidase: two distinct considerations

    Hypersensitivity

    Hyaluronidase hypersensitivity is documented.33

    Prior injections and venom sensitization are possible risk factors.33

    Recombinant human hyaluronidase provides an alternative to animal-testicular extracts, while retaining labeled hypersensitivity precautions.33,32

    The HYLENEX prescribing information distinguishes allergic reactions from anaphylactic-like reactions.32

    Alpha-gal relevance

    Theoretical concern only.

    N-glycans have been identified in purified sheep testicular hyaluronidase. This finding alone establishes neither alpha-gal content nor clinical AGS risk.36,38

    In confirmed AGS, a reaction still cannot be assumed to be α-gal-mediated.

    Theoretical α-gal evidence is not a statement about overall hyaluronidase risk.

    When a substitute is available

    • Hemostasis: verified non-gelatin options (e.g., oxidized regenerated cellulose) when clinically appropriate.
    • Fillers: source-verified hyaluronic acid (HA), calcium hydroxylapatite (CaHA), poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA), or autologous fat rather than mammalian collagen.
    • Hyaluronidase hypersensitivity is documented. Prior injections and venom sensitization are possible risk factors. Recombinant human hyaluronidase provides an alternative to animal-testicular extracts, while retaining labeled hypersensitivity precautions.33,32 See Hyaluronidase: two distinct considerations.
    • Sutures/grafts: synthetic, human, or autologous options when feasible.

    4Mechanistic context

    The established pathway is tick-induced alpha-gal-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) reacting to a later mammalian exposure.

    A hypothesized product-associated sensitization pathway remains unestablished.

    Lone-star tick (Amblyomma americanum) priming is directed at the oligosaccharide, so conventional meat IgE may be negative while gelatin, collagen, heparin, and some excipients still react.1,9

    Solid border: human clinical evidence. Dashed border: hypothesis.

    Established type I / immunoglobulin E (IgE) pathway

    Tick → α-gal IgE → Mammalian product → Reaction

    Pre-existing α-gal sensitization · human clinical evidence1,3-5

    HYPOTHESIZED · possible product-associated sensitization

    Xenogeneic product ⇢ New α-gal IgE? ⇢ Future reaction?

    •Methods, Limitations, and Disclosures
    Scope
    This companion summarizes selected literature and product-label information relevant to alpha-gal syndrome in cosmetic and aesthetic procedures. It distinguishes clinical evidence, mechanistic findings, and composition-based concerns. The management tiers are author-proposed precautions.
    Reference relationships
    The companion provides an expanded reference list and a crosswalk to the poster’s selected references. Hyaluronidase is cited to Yang (36) and Lee (38) for the distinction between N-glycans and alpha-gal identification, Guliyeva (33) for documented hypersensitivity and possible risk factors, and the HYLENEX label (32) for the recombinant alternative and labeled hypersensitivity precautions.
    Limitations
    The evidence base is dominated by case reports and cross-specialty or mechanistic extrapolation.

    Use of brand names

    Trade names appear only as illustrations of a class. Where a class is named, examples from more than one manufacturer are listed when more than one marketed product exists. Inclusion is not an endorsement; exclusion is not a judgment against a product. Brand-level α-gal content was not assayed.

    Composition and availability change over time

    Manufacturers reformulate products, change source species or excipients, and enter or leave the market. Named examples (e.g., gelatin hemostats such as GELFOAM and FLOSEAL; matrices such as Strattice and SurgiMend; testicular hyaluronidase such as Vitrase and Amphadase/Hydase versus recombinant Hylenex) reflect composition understood as of August 2026 and may be outdated. Before use, confirm the current species/source, excipients, and lot against the product’s current U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved label or Instructions for Use.
